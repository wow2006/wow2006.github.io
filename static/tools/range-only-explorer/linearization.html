Linearize, then solve

Near a point, a residual looks like a straight line: r(x + δ) ≈ r(x) + J δ. Part 1 shows how good that is; part 2 uses it to take a step.

1. Zoom in: a curve looks like its tangent

We stand at p = (1, 10) and step a distance δ in the direction (0.6, −0.8), as in the post's script. Left: the residual of beacon 3 along that line, its tangent (the linear model), and the gap between them. Right: the gap for all four residuals, ‖r(p + δ) − (r(p) + J δ)‖, on log scales.

2. Use the model: one step to its minimum

Put the linear model into the cost: F(x + δ) ≈ ½‖r(x) + J δ‖². That is a linear least-squares problem in δ, so its contours are exact ellipses (dashed) and its minimum is one solve away. The blue arrow jumps there.

  1. Part 1: shrink |δ| by 10 (one notch of 1 on the log scale). The gap shrinks by 100: the dots fall on the slope-2 line. That is what “first order” means.
  2. Part 2: from the far start, the model's ellipses are straight, but the real valley is curved. The model promises a lower cost, but the real cost goes up. Press “Take the step” and watch it recover anyway.
  3. Press “Start near”. Close to the minimum, the ellipses hug the real contours, and two or three steps land on the star. Repeating this is Gauss-Newton (post 03); fixing the far-start overshoot is Levenberg-Marquardt (post 04).
